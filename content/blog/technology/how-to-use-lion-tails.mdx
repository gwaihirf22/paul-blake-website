That was the experiment. **Lion Tails** is what it turned into: a real app, running on a server in my house, that writes Christian bedtime stories starring my kids.

## Start with a character, not a story

Most AI story tools ask you for a prompt. Lion Tails asks you for a **person**.

You make a character once — their name, what they look like, what they're like, what they're good at — and then they're reusable. Every story you write can star them. The app remembers them between stories, which is the whole point: a five-year-old doesn't want *a* story, they want a story about *them*, again, with the same hair and the same rabbit.

A character can be a boy, a girl, a dragon, a robot, or a space whale. The app asks what *kind* of thing they are and uses the right word — fur, feathers, scales, plating — rather than assuming everyone is a human child with hair.

Nothing is required except a name. Everything you add is something the story can use, and everything you leave blank is something it won't invent.

## Then write the story

On **Create Story** you pick who's in it — up to eight, and the first one is the main character — and then, optionally, everything else: how long, what it's about, what should happen.

The most useful box on that page is **"What should happen in this story?"** It's optional, and it's the difference between a generic story and one that lands. *"She was frightened of the thunderstorm last night"* produces a completely different bedtime than leaving it blank.

You can also set it somewhere real. Two ways in:

- **Alongside** — your character was always there. They help, they push back, the real person stays on their real mission.
- **A Quest with the Timekeeper** — the one I'll come back to below.

<Callout type="info">
**Longer stories take longer to write.** A story is written a chapter at a time, so the length *is* the wait — a long one is several minutes, and more on the stronger models. Nothing is broken while that happens. You can close the page; it keeps going and lands in My Stories when it's done.
</Callout>

## Quests of the Timekeeper

This is the part I've spent the most time on, and the part my kids ask for.

A quest starts in your character's own life, doing something ordinary — waiting for someone who said "just a minute", being tempted to snap at a sibling, stuck with a promise that's become inconvenient. Then a second-hand shop appears somewhere a shop couldn't be: the end of a corridor, a gap between two buildings that has only ever been a brick wall.

Only they can see it.

Inside is **Mr Barnabas**, who lends them a lantern and won't explain it. Light it and it opens onto a story — a real one. Joseph in Egypt. Daniel and the lions. Corrie ten Boom in a church in Munich in 1947. Your character walks into the account and is *part* of it: they speak, they're spoken to, they carry water, they argue for someone and lose.

What they can't do is change what happened. The account runs exactly as it's recorded — that's a hard rule, and every story ends with a plain note saying which character was invented, so a child can always tell what was real.

<Image

That picture is doing something worth pointing out. The app doesn't describe your character to an image model and hope — a description gets you a different child every time. It sends the character's own portrait as a reference, so the boy in the picture is the boy on the sheet, every time.

<Callout type="info">
**Quests need room.** They tell two stories — getting there, and the real account they visit — so they need the Long length or more. The shorter options are turned off when you switch a quest on, and the form says why.
</Callout>

## Pictures inside the story

Once a story is written, you can illustrate it.

There's a picture at the end of every story. Beyond that, open a story, hit **Make a picture**, highlight any passage, and it draws *that moment* and sets it into the text with the words running around it — a picture book rather than a story with a cover. Tap any picture to see it full size.

Every picture a story has had is kept in a strip beneath it. Drawing a new one never throws the old one away — sometimes the first one was better — and deleting one asks first.

## Parent Mode

Some things shouldn't be one tap away from a seven-year-old. **Parent Mode** is a password prompt that unlocks them for thirty minutes, or until you turn it off:

- editing a story's title or text by hand
- writing something into a character that isn't on the app's own list
- renaming a world or editing what it remembers

Two things are worth knowing. A parent's edit is **logged** — the story says "Edited by a parent" so nobody has to wonder whether the app or a person wrote a line. And the disclaimer at the end of a story about real events **cannot be edited away**; the app re-attaches it every time.

## Worlds, so stories remember each other

Tick **"I might write more stories in this world"** and the app keeps track of who appeared and what happened. The next story in that world stays true to the last one — same names, same facts, threads left open.

That's how a quest becomes a series. One journey is one story; a character who keeps going back builds something longer than any single story can hold.

## What it's built on

Briefly, for anyone who cares: TypeScript throughout, an Express server that also serves a React SPA, Postgres with Drizzle, Tailwind and Radix. Stories come from OpenAI's current text models, pictures from `gpt-image-2`. It runs in Docker on my own Unraid server behind a reverse proxy, deployed by GitHub Actions on a self-hosted runner — the same pipeline I wrote about [building with an AI assistant](/blog/technology/building-cicd-pipeline-with-ai).

The whole thing is open: **[github.com/gwaihirf22/lion-tails](https://github.com/gwaihirf22/lion-tails)**.
